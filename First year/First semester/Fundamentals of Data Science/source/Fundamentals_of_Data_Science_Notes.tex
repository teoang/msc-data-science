\begin{frameddefn}{Data Science}\label{def:data-science}
Data Science is an interdisciplinary field that extracts knowledge from
data to support decisions. It combines statistics, mathematics, computer
science, and domain expertise to collect, clean, manage, and analyse data.
\end{frameddefn}

\section{Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning}

\emph{Artificial Intelligence} (AI) studies how to build systems that
perform tasks requiring abilities such as reasoning, learning, and
problem solving.

\begin{frameddefn}{Machine Learning}\label{def:machine-learning}
Machine Learning (ML) studies algorithms whose performance $P$ on a task
$T$ improves through experience $E$. A learning problem therefore
specifies the task, the performance measure, and the available experience.
\end{frameddefn}

The main learning settings are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Supervised learning:} learn from inputs paired with known
        desired outputs to predict the output for new inputs.
  \item \textbf{Unsupervised learning:} discover structure in data without
        given target outputs.
  \item \textbf{Reinforcement learning:} learn how to act through
        interaction with an environment and feedback in the form of rewards.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{assets/artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning.png}
  \par\smallskip
  {\small\textit{AI, ML, and learning settings. Source: Spinelli, Lecture 00.}}
\end{center}

\emph{Deep learning} uses neural networks with multiple layers and can
be applied in each of these settings.

\section{Supervised models and prediction tasks}

\begin{frameddefn}{Supervised learning model}\label{def:supervised-model}
A supervised learning model is a function
$f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}$ that maps an input $x$ to a predicted
output $\hat{y}=f(x)$. Here, $\mathcal{X}$ is the input space and
$\mathcal{Y}$ is the output space. Training means choosing or adjusting
$f$ using examples of inputs paired with their known outputs.
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{frameddefn}{Regression}\label{def:regression}
Regression is a supervised learning task whose target is a real-valued
number or vector. For example, predicting a person's height from their
age is a regression task.
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{frameddefn}{Classification}\label{def:classification}
Classification is a supervised learning task whose target is a category
from a finite set of classes. For example, predicting whether a flower
is setosa, versicolor, or virginica is a classification task.
\end{frameddefn}

\section{The Iris dataset}

The Iris dataset contains 150 flowers, divided into three species:
50 setosa, 50 versicolor, and 50 virginica. The goal is to predict a
flower's species from its measurements.

\begin{framedexample}{Iris as a supervised classification task}
Each \textbf{observation} is one flower, described by four
\textbf{features}, all measured in centimetres:
\begin{itemize}
  \item sepal length and sepal width;
  \item petal length and petal width.
\end{itemize}
The \textbf{target} is the flower's species. This is supervised learning
because the species is known for the training examples, and
classification because the target is a category.
\end{framedexample}

\section{Features and targets}

In a tabular dataset, each row describes one observation. The feature
columns contain its input measurements, while the target column contains
the output we want to predict.

For the first flower shown in the lecture's table,
\[
  x=(4.3,\;3.0,\;1.1,\;0.1),
  \qquad y=\text{setosa}.
\]
The entries of $x$ are, in order, sepal length, sepal width, petal length,
and petal width, all in centimetres.

During training, both $x$ and $y$ are known. For a new input $x$, the model
produces a prediction $\hat{y}=f(x)$. The observed target $y$ and the
predicted target $\hat{y}$ may differ.

\section{Encoding categorical features}

How can we give a colour to a model that works with numbers? Many learning
algorithms require numerical features, so we need an \emph{encoding}: a
way to represent a category using numbers.

We could assign the codes red $=0$, green $=1$, and blue $=2$. But a model
that treats these codes as quantities could interpret blue as greater
than green, or as twice as far from red as green is. Colours have no
such order, and these numerical distances have no meaning here.

\emph{One-hot encoding} replaces the colour column with three yes/no
questions: ``Is it red?'', ``Is it green?'', and ``Is it blue?''.
We use $1$ for yes and $0$ for no.

\begin{framedexample}{Encoding a colour feature}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Colour} & \textbf{Is it red?} & \textbf{Is it green?} & \textbf{Is it blue?} \\
\midrule
Red   & $1$ & $0$ & $0$ \\
Green & $0$ & $1$ & $0$ \\
Blue  & $0$ & $0$ & $1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example, a green product is represented by $(0,1,0)^\top$.
Exactly one entry is $1$ and all the others are $0$: this is why the
encoding is called \emph{one-hot}.
\end{framedexample}

A model predicting a product's price can then learn a separate effect
for each colour, without assuming that blue is ``twice'' green.
The cost is more columns: one feature with $k$ categories becomes $k$
numerical columns. We can now write this idea formally.

\begin{frameddefn}{One-hot encoding}\label{def:one-hot}
For a feature with $k$ mutually exclusive categories, one-hot encoding
represents category $C_i$ by a vector $e_i\in\{0,1\}^{k}$ whose $i$-th
entry is $1$ and whose other entries are $0$:
\[
  (e_i)_j=\begin{cases}
    1,&j=i,\\
    0,&j\neq i.
  \end{cases}
\]
Each observation belongs to exactly one category, so its encoded vector
has exactly one nonzero entry.
\end{frameddefn}

When categories have a natural order, an \emph{ordinal encoding} can
preserve it. For example, low, medium, and high can be coded as $0,1,2$.
This expresses their order; it does not establish that the gaps between
successive categories are equal.

\section{Labels}

\begin{frameddefn}{Labels and labeled data}\label{def:labels}
A \emph{label} is an observed target value. In \emph{labeled data}, the
input and its target are both available.\newline
In \emph{unlabeled data}, the

\end{frameddefn}

In Iris, a flower with measurements and a known species is labeled;
the same measurements without the species are unlabeled. A label can
also be numerical, as in a regression task.

\section{Dataset notation}

Let $n$ be the number of observations and $p$ the number of numerical
features after any encoding. Write $\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}$
for the input space and $\mathcal{Y}$ for the target space.

\begin{frameddefn}{Supervised dataset}\label{def:dataset}
A labeled dataset is an indexed collection of input--target pairs,
\[
  D=\bigl((x^{(1)},y^{(1)}),\ldots,(x^{(n)},y^{(n)})\bigr)
    \in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}.
\]
The $i$-th observation has an input column vector
\[
  x^{(i)}=(x_1^{(i)},\ldots,x_p^{(i)})^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
\]
and a target $y^{(i)}\in\mathcal{Y}$.
\end{frameddefn}

The superscript $(i)$ indexes an observation; the subscript $j$ indexes
a feature. Across all observations, the $j$-th feature column is
\[
  \mathbf{x}_j=(x_j^{(1)},\ldots,x_j^{(n)})^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
\]
Thus an input vector contains $p$ features of one observation, while a
feature column contains $n$ values of one feature.

\begin{framedexample}{A small supervised dataset}
Suppose we predict house prices from area and room count. Here is a toy dataset:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{House $i$} & \textbf{Area ($\mathrm{m}^2$)} & \textbf{Rooms} & \textbf{Price (EUR)} \\
\midrule
$1$ & $50$  & $2$ & $150\,000$ \\
$2$ & $80$  & $3$ & $240\,000$ \\
$3$ & $100$ & $4$ & $300\,000$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Here $n=3$ houses and $p=2$ input features. The price is the target,
so it is not counted in $p$. For the first house,
\[
  x^{(1)}=(50,2)^\top,
  \qquad y^{(1)}=150\,000.
\]
$D$ collects the three input--target pairs. Here $x_2^{(1)}=2$ is the
first house's room count. The rooms column is
\[
  \mathbf{x}_2=(2,3,4)^\top.
\]
The vector $x^{(1)}$ describes one house using both features; the vector
$\mathbf{x}_2$ contains one feature for all three houses.
\end{framedexample}

\section{Data generation and the i.i.d. assumption}

We model the observations as realizations of random pairs $(X,Y)$
with an unknown joint probability distribution $P_{XY}$ on
$\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$. Capital letters denote random variables;
lowercase letters denote their observed values. Supervised learning
uses these observations to learn a predictive relationship between
input and target.

\begin{frameddefn}{I.i.d. observations}
\label{def:iid}
The observations are \emph{i.i.d.} when their random pairs
$(X^{(1)},Y^{(1)}),\ldots,(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})$ are mutually independent
and all have the same distribution $P_{XY}$.
\end{frameddefn}

Independence is between observations; it does not require $X$ and $Y$
within one observation to be independent. The i.i.d. assumption can fail,
for example when successive observations form a time series.

\begin{notebox}[Notation from the next section on]
From the next section on we follow the notation of the slides, which
comes from \emph{Machine Learning Refined}. Each input has $N$
coordinates and the dataset contains $P$ points: compared with the
notation above, $N=p$ and $P=n$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $x_p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ is the $p$-th point, for $p=1,\ldots,P$.
  \item For a single vector without a point index, such as $u$ or $x$,
        the subscripts $1,\ldots,N$ number its coordinates, as in the
        axis labels $x_1,x_2$ of the figures.
  \item When both indices are needed, $x_{n,p}$ is coordinate $n$ of the
        point $x_p$.
\end{itemize}
\end{notebox}

\section{Data points as vectors}

We now focus on the inputs $x_p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$. This space
supports vector addition and multiplication by real numbers, called
\emph{scalars}. These operations act coordinate by coordinate:
\[
  u+v=(u_1+v_1,\ldots,u_N+v_N)^\top,
  \qquad
  a u=(a u_1,\ldots,a u_N)^\top.
\]
An input can be drawn as a point with those coordinates or as a
\emph{position vector}: an arrow from the origin to that point.

\subsection{Mean-centering}

\begin{frameddefn}{Mean-centered data}\label{def:mean-centering}
The sample mean input vector and the centered observations are
\[
  \bar{x}=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}x_p,
  \qquad
  \widetilde{x}_p=x_p-\bar{x}.
\]
The mean of the centered inputs is zero:
$\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\widetilde{x}_p=0$.
\end{frameddefn}

Centering subtracts each feature's sample mean from that feature. It is
reversible if we retain $\bar{x}$, since
$x_p=\widetilde{x}_p+\bar{x}$.

\begin{framedexample}{Centering three points}
For the inputs $(1,1)^\top$, $(3,1)^\top$, and $(2,4)^\top$,
\[
  \bar{x}=\left(\frac{1+3+2}{3},\frac{1+1+4}{3}\right)^\top
         =(2,2)^\top.
\]
The centered inputs are $(-1,-1)^\top$, $(1,-1)^\top$, and
$(0,2)^\top$. Their sum is $(0,0)^\top$.
\end{framedexample}

\section{Spanning sets}

\begin{frameddefn}{Span and spanning set}\label{def:span}
The \emph{span} of vectors $c_1,\ldots,c_K$ is the set of all their
linear combinations:
\[
  \operatorname{span}\{c_1,\ldots,c_K\}
  =\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{K}a_nc_n:a_1,\ldots,a_K\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.
\]
These vectors form a \emph{spanning set} of a vector space $V$ when
their span equals $V$: every vector in $V$ can be expressed using them.
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{framedexample}{Spanning the plane}
The vectors
\[
  e_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  e_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
\]
span $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, because every $(a,b)^\top$ can be written as
$a e_1+b e_2$. For example,
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}=2e_1+3e_2.
\]
\begin{center}

  \draw[step=1,black!15,very thin] (-0.5,-0.5) grid (3,4);
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!60] (-0.5,0)--(3.4,0);

\section{Basis}

\begin{frameddefn}{Linear independence}\label{def:linear-independence}
Vectors $c_1,\ldots,c_K\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ are \emph{linearly independent}
if the only scalars $a_1,\ldots,a_K\in\mathbb{R}$ satisfying
\[
  a_1c_1+\cdots+a_Kc_K=0
\]
are $a_1=\cdots=a_K=0$.
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{frameddefn}{Basis}\label{def:basis}
A \emph{basis} of a vector space is a linearly independent spanning
set. Equivalently, it is a minimal spanning set: removing any of its
vectors destroys the spanning property.
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{framedexample}{A spanning set with a redundant vector}
The pair $\{e_1,e_2\}$ is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^{2}$. Adding
$c_3=e_1+e_2$ still gives a spanning set, but it is not a basis, since
\[
  e_1+e_2-c_3=0
\]
is a linear combination with nonzero coefficients. The third vector
is redundant because the first two already generate it.
\end{framedexample}

\subsection{Representing observations in a basis}

A basis of $\mathbb{R}^{N}$ contains exactly $N$ vectors, each in
$\mathbb{R}^{N}$. For a basis $c_1,\ldots,c_N$, every input observation
has a unique representation
\[
  x_p=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n,p}c_n,
  \qquad p=1,\ldots,P.
\]
The coefficients $w_{n,p}$ are the \emph{weights}, or coordinates,
of that observation in the chosen basis. The vector
$w_p=(w_{1,p},\ldots,w_{N,p})^\top$ records its representation;
combining the basis vectors with these weights reconstructs $x_p$.

\Needspace{28\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{Coordinates in a basis}
Use the same basis for both observations:
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The two points have different weights:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  x_1&=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}=2c_1+3c_2,\\[4pt]
  x_2&=\begin{pmatrix}4\\1\end{pmatrix}=4c_1+c_2.
\end{aligned}
\]
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Observation} & \textbf{Weight of $c_1$} & \textbf{Weight of $c_2$} \\
\midrule
First ($p=1$) & $w_{1,1}=2$ & $w_{2,1}=3$ \\
Second ($p=2$) & $w_{1,2}=4$ & $w_{2,2}=1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example, $w_{2,1}=3$ means that the first observation uses three
times the second basis vector. In $w_{n,p}$, the first index $n$ identifies the
basis vector; the second index $p$ identifies the observation.
\end{framedexample}

\section{Standard basis}

\begin{frameddefn}{Standard basis}\label{def:standard-basis}
The \emph{standard basis} of $\mathbb{R}^{N}$ consists of the vectors
$c_1,\ldots,c_N$, where $c_n$ has a $1$ in position $n$ and zeros
everywhere else. In $\mathbb{R}^{2}$,
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\end{frameddefn}

Each vector points along one coordinate axis. Consequently, the weights
in this basis are the entries of the observation itself:
\[
  x_p=x_{1,p}c_1+\cdots+x_{N,p}c_N
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad w_p=x_p.
\]

\begin{framedexample}{Reading the weights from the coordinates}
For $x=(2,3)^\top$, move two units along the first axis and three along
the second:
\[
  x=2c_1+3c_2,\qquad w=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}=x.
\]

\subsection{Orthogonality, unit length, and the basis matrix}

The standard basis has two properties:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Different vectors are perpendicular:}
        $c_n^\top c_m=0$ for $n\ne m$.
  \item \textbf{Each vector has unit length:}
        $\lVert c_n\rVert_2^2=c_n^\top c_n=1$.
\end{itemize}
In $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, these follow directly from
\[
  c_1^\top c_2=1\cdot0+0\cdot1=0,\qquad
  \lVert c_1\rVert_2^2=1^2+0^2=1,
\]
and similarly $\lVert c_2\rVert_2^2=1$.

\begin{frameddefn}{Orthonormal basis}\label{def:orthonormal-basis}
A basis is \emph{orthonormal} if its vectors are mutually perpendicular
and each has length $1$.
\end{frameddefn}

How can we check whether a basis is orthonormal? We need to check the
length of every vector and whether each pair of different vectors is
perpendicular. With many vectors, drawing arrows is impractical. We
can collect all these checks in one matrix.

First, place the basis vectors in the columns of $C$:
\[
  C=[\,c_1\ \cdots\ c_N\,]
  \in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}.
\]
Now compute $C^\top C$. Each entry is a dot product between two columns
of $C$: the entry in row $n$, column $m$ is $c_n^\top c_m$.
We read the result as a table of checks:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lXc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Position} & \textbf{What it tells us} & \textbf{Required value} \\
\midrule
On the diagonal & A vector's length squared & $1$ \\
Off the diagonal & The dot product of two different vectors & $0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

If the diagonal contains only $1$s and every other entry is $0$, both
checks pass: the basis is orthonormal. That result is the identity
matrix $I_N$ (written $I_{N\times N}$ in the slides), so the complete test is
\[
  \boxed{C^\top C=I_N.}
\]
This formula is a compact way to check the lengths and perpendicularity
of all the basis vectors at once. The standard basis passes the test.

\Needspace{22\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{Using the orthonormality test}
Consider the basis
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}0.6\\0.8\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.6\\0&0.8\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both vectors have length $1$, since $1^2+0^2=1$ and
$0.6^2+0.8^2=1$. Do they also pass the perpendicularity check?
\[
  C^\top C
  =\begin{pmatrix}
     1&0\\0.6&0.8
   \end{pmatrix}
   \begin{pmatrix}1&0.6\\0&0.8\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}1&0.6\\0.6&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The diagonal $1$s confirm the unit lengths. The off-diagonal entries
are $0.6$, so the vectors are not perpendicular. Therefore
$C^\top C\ne I_2$: this basis is not orthonormal, even though both
vectors have length $1$.
\end{framedexample}

In the standard basis, the weights equal the entries of the observation:
$w=x$. For a different orthonormal basis, the same observation can have
different weights. The test $C^\top C=I_N$ checks the geometry of the
basis; the chosen basis determines the weights.

\clearpage
\section{Coordinates in an orthonormal basis}

We now know how to check a basis. How do we find the weights needed to
represent a point in that basis? For an orthonormal basis, a dot product
with each basis vector gives the answer.

We know the observation $x$ and choose the basis vectors
$c_1,\ldots,c_N$. We want to find their weights $w_1,\ldots,w_N$ so that
their weighted sum gives $x$. Put the basis vectors in the columns of
$C$ and the weights in $w$. Matrix multiplication performs that sum:
\[
  Cw=w_1c_1+\cdots+w_Nc_N=x.
\]
Thus $x=Cw$ is the compact form of a linear combination. Multiplying
both sides by $C^\top$ gives
\[
  C^\top x=C^\top Cw=I_Nw=w.
\]
Therefore the coordinates are
\[
  \boxed{w=C^\top x,\qquad w_n=c_n^\top x.}
\]
The weight $w_n$ tells us how far to move along the unit vector $c_n$.
A positive weight follows its direction; a negative weight goes in the
opposite direction. Perpendicular basis vectors do not contribute to
this dot product, so it picks out just the weight we want.

For every observation $x_p$, the same calculation gives
$w_p=C^\top x_p$. With a complete orthonormal basis, combining
these weights with the basis vectors gives the original observation:
\[
  Cw_p=CC^\top x_p=x_p.
\]
Here $C$ is square and its columns form a basis of all of
$\mathbb{R}^N$, so $CC^\top=I_N$ as well as $C^\top C=I_N$.

\clearpage
\begin{framedexample}{Finding the weights of the point $(2,3)$}
Our observation is $x=(2,3)^\top$: two units to the right and three
units up. We want to describe this same point using two different
unit directions:
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
    \quad\text{(up)},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\end{pmatrix}
    \quad\text{(left)}.
\]
These vectors are perpendicular and have length $1$, so they form an
orthonormal basis. We seek weights $w_1,w_2$ for which
$x=w_1c_1+w_2c_2$.

weights describe the same point using their respective basis vectors.

\section{Nonstandard bases}

We can describe the same vector using a different basis.
\textbf{The vector stays the same; its coordinates change.}

For a concrete example, let us choose the point $x=(5,5)^\top$.
Here $x$ is simply the vector from the origin to that point.
In the standard basis, its coordinates tell us to take 5 horizontal
steps and 5 vertical steps:
\[
  x=5\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
    +5\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Now let us describe this same point using the two vectors from the slides:
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
One step along $c_1$ moves us 2 units to the right and 1 up.
One step along $c_2$ moves us 1 unit to the right and 2 up.
The question is: \textbf{how many steps of each type do we need to
reach the same point $(5,5)$?} Fractional or negative steps are allowed.

These vectors form a basis because they are linearly independent:
in the plane, they do not lie on the same line. We can therefore reach
every point with exactly one pair of weights. A basis does not have
to be orthonormal: these two vectors are neither perpendicular nor
of unit length.

\Needspace{16\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{Representing $(5,5)$ in a different basis}
Call the number of steps along $c_1$ and $c_2$ the weights $w_1$ and
$w_2$. To reach our chosen point $x=(5,5)^\top$, we need
\[
  w_1c_1+w_2c_2=x.
\]
Matching the horizontal and vertical coordinates gives
\[
  \begin{cases}
    2w_1+w_2=5,\\
    w_1+2w_2=5.
  \end{cases}
\]
Subtracting the second equation from the first gives $w_1-w_2=0$,
so the two weights are equal. Substituting into the first equation
gives $3w_1=5$. Therefore,
\[
  w_1=w_2=\frac{5}{3},\qquad
  x=\frac{5}{3}c_1+\frac{5}{3}c_2.
\]
We can check that these steps really reach our point:
\[
  \frac53\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}
  +\frac53\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}10/3+5/3\\5/3+10/3\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}5\\5\end{pmatrix}.
\]

\Needspace{15\baselineskip}
\subsection{Encoding and decoding}

We can go from a point to its weights, or from the weights back to the
point. These two operations are called \emph{encoding} and
\emph{decoding}.

\begin{frameddefn}{Encoding and decoding}
\textbf{Encoding} means starting from a point $x$ and finding the
weights $w$ that describe it in the chosen basis.

\textbf{Decoding} means starting from those weights and adding the
corresponding amounts of the basis vectors to recover $x$.
\end{frameddefn}

Let us follow both operations using the same example.

\begin{framedexample}{From a point to its weights and back}

Keep the two steps from the previous section:
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]

\paragraph{Encoding: from the point to the weights.}
Suppose we know the point we want to reach:
$x=(5,5)^\top$. How much of $c_1$ and how much of $c_2$ do we need?
This is exactly the question we answered in the previous section:
\[
  x=\begin{pmatrix}5\\5\end{pmatrix}
  \quad\xrightarrow{\text{encoding}}\quad
  w=\begin{pmatrix}5/3\\5/3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The vector $w$ simply collects our two answers: $w_1$ tells us how
much of $c_1$ to use, and $w_2$ tells us how much of $c_2$ to use.
Here we need $5/3$ of each. Finding these weights is \emph{encoding}.

\paragraph{Decoding: from the weights back to the point.}
Now start from those same weights, $w=(5/3,5/3)^\top$.
They give us instructions: take $5/3$ of the first basis vector and
add $5/3$ of the second. Following these instructions gives
\[
  x=\frac53c_1+\frac53c_2
   =\begin{pmatrix}10/3\\5/3\end{pmatrix}
    +\begin{pmatrix}5/3\\10/3\end{pmatrix}
   =\begin{pmatrix}5\\5\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This is \emph{decoding}: we recover the point in its original
coordinates. We have returned to exactly the point we started from:
\[
  \underbrace{(5,5)^\top}_{\text{original coordinates}}
  \xrightarrow{\text{encoding}}
  \underbrace{(5/3,5/3)^\top}_{\text{weights in the new basis}}
  \xrightarrow{\text{decoding}}
  \underbrace{(5,5)^\top}_{\text{original coordinates}}.
\]

\paragraph{Where does the matrix $C$ come in?}
Put the two basis vectors in the columns of a matrix:
\[
  C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Multiplying $C$ by the weights is a compact way to say
``add the two steps'':
\[
  Cw=
  \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}w_1\\w_2\end{pmatrix}
  =w_1c_1+w_2c_2.
\]
So the same relation, $Cw=x$, describes both operations.

For \textbf{encoding}, we know $x$ and find $w$ by solving the system.

For \textbf{decoding}, we know $w$ and calculate $x$ by multiplying.
The basis $C$ tells us how to interpret the weights.

\end{framedexample}

\begin{framedexample}{Decoding a negative weight}
Now start from a different pair of weights, $w=(-2,1)^\top$.
They give us instructions: take $-2$ times the first basis vector and
add one copy of the second. Following these instructions gives
\[
  x=-2c_1+c_2
   =\begin{pmatrix}-4\\-2\end{pmatrix}
    +\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}
   =\begin{pmatrix}-3\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This is decoding again: we recover the point in its original
coordinates. These weights describe the point $x=(-3,0)^\top$:
\[
  \underbrace{(-2,1)^\top}_{\text{weights in the new basis}}
  \xrightarrow{\text{decoding}}
  \underbrace{(-3,0)^\top}_{\text{original coordinates}}.
\]
The negative weight means taking twice $c_1$ in the opposite direction:
4 units to the left and 2 down. Adding $c_2$ then moves us 1 unit to
the right and 2 up, so we end at $(-3,0)$.
\end{framedexample}

Because the chosen vectors form a basis, every point has exactly one
pair of weights, and encoding followed by decoding returns the same
point.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\section{Finding the weights by least squares}

\textbf{Which weights should we choose to reconstruct a point as well
as possible?} In the previous section, we could reach our point
exactly. Now we introduce a way to measure how far off we are.
This will let us choose the best weights even when reaching the
point exactly is impossible.

\begin{framedexample}{Trying weights and measuring the error}
Let us keep the same point and basis as before:
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  x=\begin{pmatrix}5\\5\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Put $c_1$ and $c_2$ in the columns of $C$.
Suppose we try the weights $w=(1,1)^\top$: take one copy of each
basis vector. The point we obtain is
\[
  Cw=c_1+c_2
     =\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}
      +\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}
     =\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We wanted to reach $(5,5)$, but reached $(3,3)$.
We are short by 2 units in each coordinate. Subtracting the desired
point from the reconstructed point gives
\[
  Cw-x=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix}
       -\begin{pmatrix}5\\5\end{pmatrix}
       =\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This vector tells us how far off we are on each coordinate.
To turn these errors into a single number, square them and add:
\[
  (-2)^2+(-2)^2=8.
\]
Call this number $h(w)$. It depends on the weights: changing $w$
changes the point we reach, and therefore changes the error.

We already know the weights that reach our point exactly:
$w=(5/3,5/3)^\top$. With these weights, $Cw=x$, so
\[
  h(w)=0^2+0^2=0.
\]
We cannot do better than zero.
\end{framedexample}

Why square the errors before adding them? Each squared error is
nonnegative, so errors with opposite signs cannot cancel each other.
We want a measure that is zero only when every coordinate is correct.

\begin{frameddefn}{Reconstruction error and least squares}
The \textbf{reconstruction error} is the vector $Cw-x$: the
reconstructed point minus the original point.

The \textbf{squared reconstruction error} adds up the squares of
its coordinates:
\[
  h(w)=\lVert Cw-x\rVert_2^2
      =\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigl((Cw)_n-x_n\bigr)^2.
\]
Here $(Cw)_n$ is coordinate $n$ of the reconstructed point and $x_n$
is coordinate $n$ of the original point.

\textbf{Least squares} means choosing the weights $w$ that make
this sum as small as possible.
\end{frameddefn}

The formula describes the same steps as the example: reconstruct the
point, calculate the errors on its coordinates, square them, and add.
With a complete basis, we can reach every point exactly and the
minimum error is zero. But what if we can move in only one direction?

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{Approximating a point with one direction}
Let us choose a new point, $x=(2,3)^\top$: two units to the right
and three up. This time, suppose we can use only the horizontal vector
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We have just one weight $w$, a single number. Whatever value we choose,
we obtain
\[
  Cw=wc_1=\begin{pmatrix}w\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We can move right or left, but \textbf{the second coordinate always
stays zero}. We therefore cannot reach $(2,3)$ exactly.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.9cm,y=0.9cm,font=\small]
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!40] (0,-0.3)--(0,3.7)
    node[above] {$x_2$};
  \draw[-{Stealth},NotesBlue,thick] (-0.4,0)--(3.7,0)
    node[right] {$x_1$};
  \draw[red!65!black,dashed,thick] (2,0)--(2,3)
    node[midway,right] {3 units};
  \fill (2,3) circle (2.5pt)
    node[above right] {$x=(2,3)^\top$};
  \fill[NotesBlue] (2,0) circle (2.5pt)
    node[below] {$(2,0)$};
  \node[below left] at (0,0) {$0$};
  \node[NotesBlue] at (1.7,-0.8) {Available points: $(w,0)$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

We can still choose the closest point we can reach: $(2,0)$,
directly below the desired point. To get there, choose $w=2$.
Let us see why this also gives the smallest squared error:
\[
  h(w)=\underbrace{(w-2)^2}_{\text{squared horizontal error}}
       +\underbrace{(0-3)^2}_{\text{squared vertical error}}
      =(w-2)^2+9.
\]
The $9$ remains whatever weight we choose: we have no direction
that lets us move upwards. We can, however, make the horizontal
error zero by choosing $w=2$:
\[
  h(2)=0+9=9.
\]
So \textbf{the best weight is $2$, even though the minimum error is
not zero}. Other weights add a horizontal error without reducing
the vertical one.

We can also find this weight using the derivative. Since the
constant $9$ has derivative zero,
\[
  h'(w)=2(w-2)=0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w=2.
\]
This gives the same best weight we found by looking at the picture.
\end{framedexample}

Least squares lets us find the best weights in both situations:
when they give an exact reconstruction, and when they can only give
an approximation.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\subsection{The normal equations}

\textbf{How can we find the best weights without trying them one by
one?} In the previous example, we had just one weight. We found its
best value by setting the derivative of the squared error to zero.
With several weights, we need to check how the error changes with
respect to each one.

Think of the weights as knobs: one controls how much of $c_1$ we use,
another controls how much of $c_2$ we use, and so on. We study what
happens when we turn one knob while keeping the others fixed.

Recall that a partial derivative changes one weight while holding
the others fixed. The gradient collects these partial derivatives
into one vector (see \hyperref[def:optimization-gradient]{Definition~\ref*{def:optimization-gradient}}).
Setting it to zero means setting every
one of those derivatives to zero.

For our squared error $h(w)=\lVert Cw-x\rVert_2^2$, when the columns
of $C$ are linearly independent, this procedure finds the unique
minimum. Let us see what the calculations look like with two weights.

\begin{framedexample}{Finding two weights using derivatives}
Keep the same basis and point:
\[
  C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  x=\begin{pmatrix}5\\5\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For any choice of $w_1$ and $w_2$, the reconstructed point is
\[
  Cw=\begin{pmatrix}2w_1+w_2\\w_1+2w_2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Subtract 5 from each coordinate, square the two errors, and add:
\[
  h(w_1,w_2)=(2w_1+w_2-5)^2+(w_1+2w_2-5)^2.
\]

\textbf{First, change $w_1$ while keeping $w_2$ fixed.}
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \frac{\partial h}{\partial w_1}
    &=2(2w_1+w_2-5)\cdot2+2(w_1+2w_2-5)\cdot1\\
    &=2(5w_1+4w_2-15).
\end{aligned}
\]
Setting this to zero gives $5w_1+4w_2=15$.

\textbf{Next, change $w_2$ while keeping $w_1$ fixed.}
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \frac{\partial h}{\partial w_2}
    &=2(2w_1+w_2-5)\cdot1+2(w_1+2w_2-5)\cdot2\\
    &=2(4w_1+5w_2-15).
\end{aligned}
\]
Setting this to zero gives $4w_1+5w_2=15$.

We must satisfy both equations together:
\[
  \begin{cases}
    5w_1+4w_2=15,\\
    4w_1+5w_2=15.
  \end{cases}
\]
Subtracting the second equation from the first gives $w_1-w_2=0$,
so the weights are equal. The first equation becomes $9w_1=15$:
\[
  w_1=w_2=\frac53.
\]
These are the weights we already found before. They reconstruct
$(5,5)^\top$ exactly, so the minimum squared error is zero.
\end{framedexample}

So far, our example had two coordinates and two weights. Before
we generalize, let us see what changes when the number of chosen
directions is smaller than the number of coordinates.

\begin{framedexample}{Three coordinates, two chosen directions}
Imagine a point in three-dimensional space:
\[
  x=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\4\end{pmatrix}.
\]
To reach it from the origin, move 2 units to the right, 3 forwards,
and 4 up. The three available directions are
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_3=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Using all three, we can reach the point exactly:
\[
  x=2c_1+3c_2+4c_3.
\]

\textbf{Now choose $K=2$: keep only $c_1$ and $c_2$.}
The matrix of chosen directions is
\[
  C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
It has \textbf{3 rows}, because each point has three original
coordinates, and \textbf{2 columns}, because we chose two directions.
We started with three available directions; after choosing two,
the matrix $C$ we use has only two columns.

With two weights, we can reconstruct only points of the form
\[
  Cw=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
      \begin{pmatrix}w_1\\w_2\end{pmatrix}
     =\begin{pmatrix}w_1\\w_2\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We can move right and forwards, but \textbf{we can no longer move
up}: the third coordinate always stays zero.

To get as close as possible to $(2,3,4)^\top$, choose
\[
  w=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  \hat{x}=Cw=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The hat distinguishes the reconstructed point $\hat{x}$ from the
original point $x$. Why are these weights the best? The squared
error is
\[
  h(w_1,w_2)=(w_1-2)^2+(w_2-3)^2+(0-4)^2.
\]
Choosing $w_1=2$ and $w_2=3$ makes the first two terms zero. The
third term remains, whatever weights we choose:
\[
  h(2,3)=0+0+16=16.
\]
We have described the point using \textbf{two weights instead of
three coordinates}, but lost its height. Every point we can
reconstruct lies in the horizontal plane where the third coordinate
is zero.
\end{framedexample}

So now we want to generalize. \textbf{If $C$ has $K$ columns, we
need $K$ weights}, one for each chosen direction. If the original
points have $N$ coordinates, $C$ has $N$ rows: multiplying an
$N\times K$ matrix by a $K$-entry weight vector produces an
$N$-entry reconstructed point. The three-coordinate example has
$N=3$ and $K=2$.

With $K$ weights, we set $K$ partial derivatives to zero, obtaining
one equation for each weight. We do not need to write out all the
coordinate expressions every time: the derivative calculation has
the compact matrix form
\[
  \nabla_w h(w)=2C^\top(Cw-x).
\]
Here $C^\top$ is the \emph{transpose} of $C$: its rows are the
columns of $C$. Setting the gradient to zero, dividing by 2, and
expanding gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
  C^\top(Cw-x)&=0,\\
  C^\top Cw-C^\top x&=0,\\
  C^\top Cw&=C^\top x.
\end{aligned}
\]
This last line is the system obtained from the derivatives, written
in matrix form. It is called the \emph{normal equations}.

\begin{framedprop}{Normal equations}\label{prop:normal-equations}
For a fixed matrix $C$ with linearly independent columns, the unique
weights that minimise $\lVert Cw-x\rVert_2^2$ satisfy
\[
  C^\top Cw=C^\top x.
\]
\end{framedprop}

We know $C$ and $x$, so we can calculate $C^\top C$ and $C^\top x$.
The only unknowns left are the weights in $w$.

\begin{framedexample}{The same system in matrix form}
Return to our two-dimensional basis $c_1=(2,1)^\top$,
$c_2=(1,2)^\top$ and point $x=(5,5)^\top$.
Calculate the two known parts:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  C^\top C
    &=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}
      \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}
      =\begin{pmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{pmatrix},\\[6pt]
  C^\top x
    &=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}
      \begin{pmatrix}5\\5\end{pmatrix}
      =\begin{pmatrix}15\\15\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the normal equations say
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}w_1\\w_2\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}15\\15\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Reading the first and second rows gives exactly the two equations
we obtained by differentiating. Solving them gives
$w=(5/3,5/3)^\top$ again.
\end{framedexample}

The entries of $C^\top C$ are the dot products between the chosen
directions. If the columns of $C$ are orthonormal, their dot products
make $C^\top C=I$, and the equations simplify to $w=C^\top x$.
This recovers the shortcut used earlier for orthonormal directions.

\subsection{Encoding the whole dataset}

So far, we have found the weights of one point. A dataset contains
many points. \textbf{We now repeat the same operation for every point,
using the same chosen directions.}
The matrix $C$ stays fixed; each point gets its own weights.

\begin{frameddefn}{Encoding a dataset}
To \textbf{encode a dataset} in fixed directions $C$, find a weight
vector for each observation. Write $x_p$ for observation $p$ and
$w_p$ for its weights. Each reconstructed observation is $Cw_p$.

The subscript $p$ identifies the observation. In particular, $w_1$
is now the \emph{whole weight vector of the first observation}.
Its individual entries are $w_{1,1},\ldots,w_{K,1}$, one for each
direction.
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{framedexample}{Two points, the same basis}
Keep our two basis vectors:
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Suppose our dataset contains just two observations:
\[
  x_1=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  x_2=\begin{pmatrix}6\\6\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For the first point, one copy of each basis vector is enough:
\[
  c_1+c_2=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  w_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For the second point, we need two copies of each:
\[
  2c_1+2c_2=\begin{pmatrix}6\\6\end{pmatrix}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  w_2=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We have encoded both points. The directions did not change; the
amount of each direction did.
\end{framedexample}

We can also find these weights using the normal equations.
For each observation $p$, solve
\[
  C^\top Cw_p=C^\top x_p.
\]
The left-hand matrix $C^\top C$ is the same for every point, so we
calculate it once. The right-hand side changes because $x_p$ changes.

\begin{framedexample}{Finding each point's weights}
Use the same two observations and basis as in the previous example.
The shared matrix is
\[
  C^\top C=\begin{pmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{pmatrix}.
\]

\textbf{First observation: $x_1=(3,3)^\top$.}
Compute the right-hand side:
\[
  C^\top x_1
  =\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot3+1\cdot3\\1\cdot3+2\cdot3\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}9\\9\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The system for this observation is
\[
  \begin{cases}
    5w_{1,1}+4w_{2,1}=9,\\
    4w_{1,1}+5w_{2,1}=9.
  \end{cases}
\]
Here the first subscript chooses the direction; the second chooses
the observation. Subtracting the equations gives
$w_{1,1}=w_{2,1}$. Substituting into the first gives
$9w_{1,1}=9$, hence
\[
  w_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  Cw_1=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix}=x_1.
\]

\textbf{Second observation: $x_2=(6,6)^\top$.}
Keep the same left-hand matrix, and calculate the new right-hand side:
\[
  C^\top x_2
  =\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot6+1\cdot6\\1\cdot6+2\cdot6\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}18\\18\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The system is now
\[
  \begin{cases}
    5w_{1,2}+4w_{2,2}=18,\\
    4w_{1,2}+5w_{2,2}=18.
  \end{cases}
\]
Subtracting again shows that the weights are equal.
The first equation gives $9w_{1,2}=18$, hence
\[
  w_2=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  Cw_2=\begin{pmatrix}6\\6\end{pmatrix}=x_2.
\]
We obtain the same weights as by inspecting the two points.
\end{framedexample}

\paragraph{How well do we reconstruct the whole dataset?}
Calculate the squared error for each observation, add these errors,
and divide by the number of observations.

\begin{frameddefn}{Average squared reconstruction error}
For $P$ observations, the average squared reconstruction error is
\[
  \frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\lVert Cw_p-x_p\rVert_2^2.
\]
Each term measures the squared error for one observation. The sum
adds the errors of all $P$ observations, and dividing by $P$ gives
their average.
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{framedexample}{Average error for our two observations}
Both reconstructions above are exact: $Cw_1=x_1$ and $Cw_2=x_2$.
Their squared errors are therefore both zero, so the average is
\[
  \frac{0+0}{2}=0.
\]
\end{framedexample}

For fixed $C$, changing one observation's weights affects only that
observation's error. Finding the best weights for each point
separately therefore also minimises the average error.

With a complete basis, each point still has as many weights as
original coordinates. To describe it with fewer numbers, we must
choose fewer directions. The next section explores this idea.

\section{Using fewer directions: subspaces}

We have learned how to encode every point using the same directions.
But with a complete basis, a point with two original coordinates
still needs two weights. We have changed its description without
reducing the number of values we store for each point.

\textbf{Could we describe the same observations using fewer numbers?}
To try this, we choose fewer directions. Let us return to the small
dataset we have just encoded.

\begin{framedexample}{Two coordinates, but only one weight}
Our two observations are
\[
  x_1=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  x_2=\begin{pmatrix}6\\6\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Notice what they have in common: in each point, the two coordinates
are equal. Once we know one coordinate, we already know the other.
Storing both repeats the same information.

Instead of the two directions used before, choose just the diagonal
vector
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
One step along this vector moves us 1 unit to the right and 1 up.
To reach the first point, take three steps. To reach the second,
take six:
\[
  x_1=3c_1,\qquad x_2=6c_1.
\]
We can therefore encode the first observation with the single
weight $w_1=3$ and the second with the single weight $w_2=6$.
Here the subscript identifies the observation; each weight is now
just one number.

We keep the chosen direction once for the whole dataset. For each
observation, we then need only one number instead of two. Knowing
that direction, we can still reconstruct both coordinates exactly:
\[
  3\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
    =\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  6\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
    =\begin{pmatrix}6\\6\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\end{framedexample}

\textbf{What have we given up by keeping only one direction?}
We can still reach both observations in this dataset, but we can
no longer reach every point in the plane.

\begin{framedexample}{Which points can our one direction reach?}
Keep $c_1=(1,1)^\top$. The matrix $C$ has just this one column.
With any real weight $w$, we reconstruct
\[
  Cw=w\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
     =\begin{pmatrix}w\\w\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Every reachable point has equal coordinates. Together, these points
form the diagonal line $x_2=x_1$ through the origin.
Positive weights take us to the upper right; negative weights take
us to the lower left. Weight zero gives the origin. We can move
along the whole line, in both directions.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small,x=0.65cm,y=0.65cm]
  \draw[step=1,black!12,very thin] (-2,-2) grid (6,6);
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!45] (-2.5,0)--(7.1,0) node[right] {$x_1$};
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!45] (0,-2.5)--(0,7.1) node[above] {$x_2$};
  \draw[NotesBlue,very thick] (-2.2,-2.2)--(6.6,6.6)
    node[above left] {$x_2=x_1$};
  \fill[NotesBlue] (3,3) circle (2.5pt) node[above left] {$(3,3)$};
  \fill[NotesBlue] (6,6) circle (2.5pt) node[right] {$(6,6)$};
  \fill[red!65!black] (2,0) circle (2.5pt)
    node[below right] {$(2,0)$};
  \node[below left] at (0,0) {$0$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Now try to reach a different point, $x=(2,0)^\top$.
Its first coordinate would require $w=2$, but its second would
require $w=0$. We have only one weight, so we cannot satisfy both
requirements. This point lies outside our line.
\end{framedexample}

The line is our collection of reachable points. It has a name:
a \emph{subspace}. The original observations still have two
coordinates, but moving within this line needs only one independent
number. That is why the line has dimension one.

\begin{frameddefn}{Subspace spanned by the columns}\label{def:column-subspace}
The \textbf{span} of the columns of $C$ contains all their linear combinations:
\[
  S=\operatorname{span}\{c_1,\ldots,c_K\}
   =\{Cw:w\in\mathbb{R}^K\}.
\]
These points form a \textbf{linear subspace}: a set containing the
origin and closed under vector addition and multiplication by real numbers.

If its $K$ columns are independent, they form a basis of $S$, and
$S$ has \textbf{dimension $K$}.
\end{frameddefn}

\paragraph{From the picture to $N$ coordinates and $K$ directions.}
Each original observation has $N$ coordinates. Choose $K<N$
linearly independent directions and put them in the columns of $C$:
\[
  \underbrace{C}_{N\times K}
  \underbrace{w}_{K\text{ weights}}
  =\underbrace{Cw}_{N\text{ coordinates}}.
\]
The directions still have $N$ entries each. Choosing fewer of them
reduces the number of weights, not the number of coordinates in a
reconstructed point. All reconstructions lie in the same
$K$-dimensional subspace $S$.

In the diagonal example, $N=2$ and $K=1$: our reachable set is a line.
In the earlier three-coordinate example, $N=3$ and $K=2$: keeping
the two horizontal directions gave a plane through the origin.
In each case, $K$ tells us how many independent weights we can adjust.

\begin{frameddefn}{Dimensionality reduction}
\textbf{Dimensionality reduction} represents observations with fewer
numbers. Here, we replace each observation's $N$ original coordinates
with $K<N$ weights in shared, linearly independent directions.

A point inside their span can be reconstructed exactly. A point
outside it cannot: we must settle for an approximation.
\end{frameddefn}

\paragraph{What happens to a point outside our subspace?}
For our diagonal line, $(3,3)^\top$ and $(6,6)^\top$ are recovered
exactly. The point $(2,0)^\top$ cannot be. We already know from
least squares what to do: choose the reachable point with the
smallest error. Next, we find that point on the line and give this
operation its geometric name: \emph{projection}.

\section{Projection onto a subspace}

At the end of the previous section, we had a problem: our chosen
direction let us move along a diagonal line, but the point we wanted
to describe was outside that line.

We cannot reach the original point exactly. We can still ask:
\textbf{which point on our line gets us closest?}
Let us find it, then look at what this choice means geometrically.

\begin{framedexample}{Finding the closest point on the diagonal}
Keep the direction $c_1=(1,1)^\top$ and the point $x=(2,0)^\top$.
Our matrix $C$ has one column, so we have just one weight $w$:
\[
  C=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  Cw=\begin{pmatrix}w\\w\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Every value of $w$ gives a point on the line $x_2=x_1$.
No value reaches $(2,0)$, because its two coordinates are different.
To choose the closest point, minimise the squared error:
\[
  h(w)=(w-2)^2+w^2=2(w-1)^2+2.
\]
The term $2(w-1)^2$ is nonnegative, and becomes zero at $w=1$.
So the best weight is $1$, and the minimum squared error is $2$.

Write the best weight as $w_*$ and the reconstructed point as
$\hat{x}$. The star marks our best choice; the hat distinguishes
the reconstruction from the original point:
\[
  w_*=1,\qquad
  \hat{x}=Cw_*=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small,x=1.6cm,y=1.6cm]
  \draw[step=1,black!12,very thin] (-0.5,-0.5) grid (2.5,2.5);
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!50] (-0.6,0)--(2.8,0) node[right] {$x_1$};
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!50] (0,-0.6)--(0,2.8) node[above] {$x_2$};
  \draw[NotesBlue,thick] (-0.45,-0.45)--(2.45,2.45)
    node[above left] {$S: x_2=x_1$};
  \draw[-{Stealth},black,thick] (0,0)--(2,0);
  \fill (2,0) circle (2pt) node[below right] {$x=(2,0)^\top$};
  \draw[-{Stealth},NotesBlue,very thick] (0,0)--(1,1);
  \fill[NotesBlue] (1,1) circle (2pt)
    node[above left,xshift=2mm,yshift=3mm] {$\hat{x}=(1,1)^\top$};
  \draw[-{Stealth},red!65!black,dashed,thick] (1,1)--(2,0)
    node[midway,above right] {$x-\hat{x}$};

The blue line contains every point we can reconstruct. The black
point is the original observation. The blue point is our closest
reconstruction. The dashed segment joins these last two points;
notice that it meets the line at a right angle.

We have replaced $(2,0)^\top$ with $(1,1)^\top$, the best point we
can reach using our chosen direction. This is an
\emph{orthogonal projection} onto the line.
\end{framedexample}

The same idea works with a plane or a higher-dimensional subspace:
among all the points our chosen directions can reach, keep the one
closest to the original observation.

\begin{frameddefn}{Orthogonal projection}\label{def:orthogonal-projection}
The \textbf{orthogonal projection} of $x$ onto a linear subspace
$S$ is the point $\hat{x}$ in $S$ closest to $x$.

If the linearly independent columns of $C$ span $S$, write
\[
  w_*=\argmin_w\lVert Cw-x\rVert_2^2,\qquad
  \hat{x}=Cw_*.
\]
The notation $\argmin$ returns the \emph{weights} giving the smallest
error. Multiplying by $C$ then gives the \emph{point} in the subspace.
\end{frameddefn}

\paragraph{How do we find the projection with several directions?}
Use the normal equations we already know:
\[
  C^\top Cw_*=C^\top x.
\]
First solve for the best weights $w_*$. Then decode them by computing
$\hat{x}=Cw_*$. Nothing here requires the minimum error to be zero.
The equations find the closest reconstruction even when the original
point lies outside the subspace.

\begin{framedexample}{Finding the point with normal equations}
For $C=(1,1)^\top$ and $x=(2,0)^\top$, the two known quantities are
\[
  C^\top C=1\cdot1+1\cdot1=2,\qquad
  C^\top x=1\cdot2+1\cdot0=2.
\]
So the normal equations reduce to $2w_*=2$, giving $w_*=1$.
Decoding gives $\hat{x}=(1,1)^\top$, the same closest point as before.
\end{framedexample}

There is also a compact formula. Independent columns make
$C^\top C$ invertible. Multiplying the normal equations on the left
by $(C^\top C)^{-1}$ gives
\[
  \underbrace{(C^\top C)^{-1}(C^\top C)}_{I_K}w_*
   =(C^\top C)^{-1}C^\top x.
\]
Thus the two steps, finding the weights and reconstructing the point,
can be written as
\[
  w_*=(C^\top C)^{-1}C^\top x,\qquad
  \hat{x}=C(C^\top C)^{-1}C^\top x.
\]
These are compact expressions for the same operations. We can find
the weights by solving the normal equations directly.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{Why the projection is perpendicular}

Look again at the dashed segment from the reconstructed point to
the original point. It represents what is left over after using
our chosen directions.

\begin{frameddefn}{Residual}
The \textbf{residual} is the vector from the reconstruction to the
original point:
\[
  r=x-\hat{x}=x-Cw_*.
\]
It has the opposite sign to the earlier error vector $Cw_*-x$,
but the same squared length. The squared reconstruction error is
therefore $\lVert r\rVert_2^2$.
\end{frameddefn}

Why does this vector meet the subspace at a right angle?
The normal equations give us the answer:
\[
  C^\top r=C^\top x-C^\top Cw_*=0.
\]
Each row of $C^\top$ is one chosen direction transposed. This equation
therefore says that $c_k^\top r=0$ for every chosen direction $c_k$.
A zero dot product means perpendicularity: $r$ is perpendicular to
each direction, and hence to every vector in their span.
If $r=0$, the reconstruction is already exact.

\begin{framedexample}{Checking the right angle}
For our diagonal line, $\hat{x}=(1,1)^\top$ and $x=(2,0)^\top$.
The part left over is
\[
  r=x-\hat{x}
   =\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix}
    -\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
   =\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Check its dot product with the direction of the line:
\[
  c_1^\top r=(1,1)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}=1-1=0.
\]
The residual is perpendicular to the line. Its squared length is
$1^2+(-1)^2=2$, matching the minimum squared error we found earlier.
\end{framedexample}

Imagine choosing another point $y$ in the subspace. Starting from
$\hat{x}$, reaching $y$ adds a sideways displacement within $S$.
That displacement is perpendicular to $r$. By Pythagoras,
\[
  \lVert x-y\rVert_2^2
  =\lVert x-\hat{x}\rVert_2^2+\lVert\hat{x}-y\rVert_2^2.
\]
Any different point adds a positive term to the distance squared.
This is why the perpendicular projection is the closest point.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{Encoding and decoding the projection}

The two operations still follow the same order: find the weights,
then use them to reconstruct a point. The change is that decoding
now returns the closest point in $S$, which may differ from $x$.

\begin{framedexample}{Encoding and decoding one point}
Start with our original point $x=(2,0)^\top$ and the fixed direction
$C=(1,1)^\top$.

\textbf{Encoding.} Solve the normal equations $2w_*=2$.
We obtain and store just one number: $w_*=1$.

\textbf{Decoding.} Follow that weight's instruction: take one copy
of the chosen direction. This gives
\[
  \hat{x}=C\cdot1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The full journey is
\[
  \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{original: 2 coordinates}}
  \xrightarrow{\text{encode}}
  \underbrace{1}_{\text{1 weight}}
  \xrightarrow{\text{decode}}
  \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{reconstructed: 2 coordinates}}.
\]
We return to two coordinates, but we do not recover the original
point: the residual $r=(1,-1)^\top$ was left out.

Suppose instead we started from $(1,1)^\top$, already on the line.
It would also be encoded with weight $1$, and decoding would return
it exactly. The same stored weight can therefore come from different
original points. It records where we end up on the line, without
recording the part perpendicular to it.
\end{framedexample}

With $K$ chosen independent directions in $N$ dimensions, encoding
stores $K$ weights and decoding produces $N$ coordinates in their
span. Points already in that span are recovered exactly; other
points are replaced by their projections.

\clearpage
\section{Orthonormal directions in a subspace}

We now know how to find the closest point in a chosen subspace:
solve the normal equations, then reconstruct the point using the
weights. Can we make the calculation simpler?

Yes: choose directions that are \textbf{mutually perpendicular and
have length one}. These are the orthonormal directions introduced
in \hyperref[def:orthonormal-basis]{Definition~\ref*{def:orthonormal-basis}}.
We can use them as a basis of a smaller subspace, without needing
a basis of the whole original space.

Let us first return to our diagonal line. We can change the length
of the vector we use to describe it without changing the line itself.

\begin{framedexample}{The same line, a unit direction}
We still want to approximate $x=(2,0)^\top$ on the line $x_2=x_1$.
Previously we used the direction $(1,1)^\top$.
Its length is $\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2$. Divide by that length to get
a unit vector:
\[
  c_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad C=c_1.
\]
This vector points along the same diagonal, but one copy of it is
a shorter step than one copy of $(1,1)^\top$.

With a unit direction, its dot product with $x$ gives the best weight:
\[
  w_*=c_1^\top x
      =\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot2+\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot0
      =\sqrt2.
\]
To decode, take $\sqrt2$ copies of the new direction:
\[
  \hat{x}=Cw_*=
    \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\sqrt2
    =\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We reach the same projected point as before. The old direction
needed weight $1$; the shorter unit direction needs weight $\sqrt2$.
\textbf{The weight changes because the size of one step changes.}
The line and the closest point stay the same.
\end{framedexample}

\paragraph{Why does the dot product give the weight directly?}
Recall that $C^\top C$ collects the dot products between the columns
of $C$. With orthonormal columns, each vector's dot product with
itself is $1$, and the dot product of two different columns is $0$.
For $K$ columns, this gives $C^\top C=I_K$.

Substitute this into the normal equations:
\[
  C^\top Cw_*=C^\top x
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  I_Kw_*=C^\top x
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  w_*=C^\top x.
\]
There is no system left to solve: each weight can be calculated
separately as $w_{*,k}=c_k^\top x$. It measures the signed amount
of $x$ along unit direction $c_k$.

\begin{framedprop}{Orthonormal projection formulas}
If the columns of $C\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}$ are orthonormal,
the best weights and closest reconstruction are
\[
  \underbrace{w_*=C^\top x}_{\text{encoding}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\hat{x}=Cw_*=CC^\top x}_{\text{decoding}}.
\]
Encoding takes a dot product with each chosen direction. Decoding
multiplies each direction by its weight and adds the contributions.
\end{framedprop}

\paragraph{The two matrix products have different jobs.}
The order matters. $C^\top C$ acts on the $K$ weights and equals
$I_K$. The product $CC^\top$ acts on the original $N$ coordinates:
it first encodes the point and then decodes its weights.

\begin{frameddefn}{Orthogonal projection matrix}\label{def:orthogonal-projection-matrix}
For orthonormal columns of $C$, the matrix $CC^\top$ is the
\textbf{orthogonal projection matrix} onto their span $S$.
Multiplying an original point by it returns the closest point in $S$:
\[
  \hat{x}=CC^\top x.
\]
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{framedexample}{Two products, two different results}
For our unit diagonal direction,
\[
  C=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
\]
has two rows and one column. The product $C^\top C$ is a single entry:
\[
  C^\top C=\frac12+\frac12=1.
\]
This is $I_1$, confirming that the direction has unit length.
But reversing the order produces a $2\times2$ matrix:
\[
  CC^\top=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
It is not $I_2$. It projects points onto our line:
\[
  CC^\top\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix}
  =\frac12\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This multiplication combines the two operations we just performed:
find the weight $\sqrt2$, then use it to reconstruct $(1,1)^\top$.
\end{framedexample}

Orthonormality simplifies the calculation; it does not add any
missing directions. If $K<N$, the projection can still leave out
part of the original point. With a complete orthonormal basis
($K=N$), every point can be recovered, and $CC^\top=I_N$ as well.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{From three coordinates to two}

Let us apply the shortcut to our earlier three-coordinate example.
We kept two directions and lost the height of the point. Those directions were already perpendicular unit vectors,
so we can now find their weights directly by dot products.

\begin{framedexample}{Two weights for a point in space}
Our point is $x=(2,3,4)^\top$: 2 units to the right, 3 forwards,
and 4 up. Keep only the first two directions:
\[
  c_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
They are perpendicular and each has length $1$. Their combinations
form the horizontal plane $x_3=0$.

\textbf{Encoding: find the two weights.}
Take the dot product with each direction:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  w_{*,1}&=c_1^\top x=1\cdot2+0\cdot3+0\cdot4=2,\\
  w_{*,2}&=c_2^\top x=0\cdot2+1\cdot3+0\cdot4=3.
\end{aligned}
\]
Together, these give
\[
  w_*=C^\top x=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We store two numbers, telling us to use $c_1$ twice and $c_2$ three times.

\textbf{Decoding: follow those instructions.}
\[
  \hat{x}=Cw_*=2c_1+3c_2=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The reconstructed point has three coordinates again, but its height
is zero. The directions we kept cannot move us upwards.
The part left out is
\[
  r=x-\hat{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\4\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \lVert r\rVert_2^2=16.
\]
This is the same projection and error we found before. Orthonormality
has made finding the weights easier.

Here the two matrix products are especially easy to read:
\[
  C^\top C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=I_2,
  \qquad
  CC^\top=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The projection matrix keeps the first two coordinates and replaces
the third with zero.
\end{framedexample}

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection{What changes when we reduce the dimension?}

There are two separate choices. \textbf{How many directions we keep}
determines which points we can reconstruct exactly.
\textbf{Whether those directions are orthonormal} determines whether
we can use the dot-product shortcut.

For linearly independent columns, the cases are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Directions} & \textbf{Finding the weights} & \textbf{Reconstruction} \\
\midrule
Complete basis & Solve the normal equations. & Exact for every point. \\
Complete, orthonormal & $w_*=C^\top x$. & Exact for every point. \\
Basis of a subspace & Solve the normal equations. & Closest point in $S$. \\
Orthonormal in a subspace & $w_*=C^\top x$. & Closest point in $S$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

With fewer directions, points inside $S$ are still recovered exactly.
Points outside it are replaced by their projections; the perpendicular
residual is lost. Averaging the squared lengths of these residuals
gives the dataset's average squared reconstruction error.

So far, we have chosen the directions first and then found the best
weights. Next, we ask how to choose the directions themselves from
the data.

\section{Why learn directions from the data?}

We have learned how to describe points using fewer directions.
Until now, we chose those directions first, then found the best
weights for each point. But one question remains:
\textbf{which directions should we choose?}

The observations themselves can give us a clue. If they follow a
particular pattern, a direction that follows that pattern may let us
describe them with fewer numbers while losing little information.
Let us look at a small dataset where we can see this immediately.

\begin{framedexample}{Three points, one line}\label{ex:pca-three-points}
Suppose each observation has two features, and our three observations are
\[
  x_1=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  x_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  x_3=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Their mean is $(0,0)^\top$, so they are already centred.

Notice the pattern: in every observation, the two feature values
are equal. Once we know one value, we already know the other.
These two features repeat the same information.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small,x=0.65cm,y=0.65cm]
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!40] (-3,0)--(3.2,0) node[right] {feature 1};
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!40] (0,-3)--(0,3.2) node[above] {feature 2};
  \draw[NotesBlue,thick] (-2.8,-2.8)--(2.8,2.8);
  \fill[NotesBlue] (-2,-2) circle (2.5pt)
    node[below right] {$(-2,-2)$};
  \fill[NotesBlue] (0,0) circle (2.5pt) node[below right] {$(0,0)$};
  \fill[NotesBlue] (2,2) circle (2.5pt) node[above left] {$(2,2)$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

All three points lie on the same diagonal line through the origin.
The negative point and the positive point are on opposite sides
of the origin, but they belong to the same line.

Choose the unit diagonal direction we used in the previous chapter:
\[
  c_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We need just one weight for each point, telling us how far to move
along that direction:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Original observation} & \textbf{Weight $w_p=c_1^\top x_p$} \\
\midrule
$(-2,-2)^\top$ & $-2\sqrt2$ \\
$(0,0)^\top$ & $0$ \\
$(2,2)^\top$ & $2\sqrt2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The negative weight moves in the opposite direction; the zero
weight stays at the origin. Decoding recovers the two coordinates
exactly. For the last point,
\[
  c_1w_3=
  \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}2\sqrt2
  =\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The direction is shared by all observations. Once we keep it,
each observation needs one weight instead of two feature values.
\end{framedexample}

\paragraph{Why choose the diagonal?}
We chose it because of the pattern in the data. A horizontal direction
would also let us store one weight per point, but it would discard
the second coordinate of the two nonzero observations. The diagonal
lets us keep both coordinates exactly.

\textbf{Using fewer numbers is only half the task: we must also
choose directions that preserve the information in the observations.}
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) makes this choice from the data.
It uses the observations together to find shared directions that
keep as much of their variation as possible. In our example, all
the movement between observations happens along the diagonal.

Real observations often lie close to a line or plane without lying
exactly on it. Then some information must be lost. PCA looks for
directions that make the average squared reconstruction error as
small as possible. We will write this choice precisely in the next
section.

\paragraph{What if the cloud is away from the origin?}
The lines and planes we have used so far pass through the origin.
A real cloud of observations can sit elsewhere. Before choosing
PCA directions, we move the cloud so that its mean is at the origin.
This is the mean-centring introduced in
\hyperref[def:mean-centering]{Definition~\ref*{def:mean-centering}}.
It preserves the differences between observations while removing
their common offset.

\begin{framedexample}{The same pattern away from the origin}
Suppose the original observations are now
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}8\\18\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  \begin{pmatrix}10\\20\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  \begin{pmatrix}12\\22\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Their mean is $\mu=(10,20)^\top$. Subtracting $\mu$ from each point
gives exactly the three centred observations in the first example.
For the last point,
\[
  x_3=\begin{pmatrix}12\\22\end{pmatrix}
       -\begin{pmatrix}10\\20\end{pmatrix}
      =\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We can encode this centred point with the same diagonal direction
and weight $w_3=2\sqrt2$. Decoding gives $(2,2)^\top$.
To return to the original location, add the mean back:
\[
  \mu+c_1w_3
   =\begin{pmatrix}10\\20\end{pmatrix}
    +\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}
   =\begin{pmatrix}12\\22\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The mean and direction are kept once for the dataset; each observation
still needs only its own weight. All the $x_p$ used in the following
PCA calculations refer to the centred observations.
\end{framedexample}

We can now separate the two choices clearly: for any fixed directions,
we know how to find the best weights. PCA also chooses the directions.
Next, we compare those choices through their reconstruction errors.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\section{Choosing directions by reconstruction error}

In the previous section, the diagonal looked like a good direction
because it followed the pattern in our data. Now we want a numerical
way to compare it with other choices.

The idea is to \textbf{reconstruct the same observations using each
candidate set of directions, then compare the errors}.
For each choice, we use the best possible weights. This way, we judge
the directions themselves, rather than a poor choice of weights.

\begin{framedexample}{The same data, two choices of direction}\label{ex:pca-reconstruction-choice}
Keep the three centred observations from
\hyperref[ex:pca-three-points]{Example~\ref*{ex:pca-three-points}}:
\[
  x_1=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  x_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  x_3=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We want just one weight per observation, so we keep one direction.
Compare these two unit vectors:
\[
  c_h=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
  \quad\text{(horizontal)},\qquad
  c_d=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
  \quad\text{(diagonal)}.
\]
For each choice, $C$ contains just that one column. Both choices
use the same number of weights; the direction is what changes.

\textbf{First, try the horizontal direction.}
For the point $x_3=(2,2)^\top$, the best weight is
\[
  w_3=c_h^\top x_3=2.
\]
Decoding takes two horizontal steps:
\[
  \hat{x}_3=c_hw_3=\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We kept the first coordinate but lost the second. The squared error is
\[
  \lVert\hat{x}_3-x_3\rVert_2^2
   =(2-2)^2+(0-2)^2=4.
\]
The negative point also loses its second coordinate and has squared
error $4$. The origin is reconstructed exactly, so its error is $0$.

\textbf{Now try the diagonal direction.}
For the same point, the best weight is
\[
  w_3=c_d^\top x_3=2\sqrt2.
\]
Decoding gives
\[
  \hat{x}_3=c_dw_3=
  \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}2\sqrt2
  =\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This reconstruction is exact. The other two observations also lie
on the diagonal, so their squared errors are zero too.

To compare the choices using the whole dataset, average the squared
errors of all three observations:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Observation} & \textbf{Horizontal error} & \textbf{Diagonal error} \\
\midrule

The entries are squared errors. Their averages are
\[
  \text{horizontal: }\frac{4+0+4}{3}=\frac83,
  \qquad
  \text{diagonal: }\frac{0+0+0}{3}=0.
\]
The diagonal wins: it uses one weight per observation, just like
the horizontal choice, but reconstructs every observation exactly.
\end{framedexample}

\paragraph{What does PCA choose for a larger dataset?}
The same comparison applies when the points have many features and
we keep more than one direction. Write $P$ for the number of
observations, $N$ for their original number of coordinates, and
$K<N$ for the number of directions we keep.

Put the shared directions in the columns of
$C\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}$. Each centred observation $x_p$ gets
its own $K$-entry weight vector $w_p$. Decoding gives $Cw_p$,
which we compare with $x_p$.

\Needspace{14\baselineskip}
\begin{frameddefn}{Principal Component Analysis}\label{def:pca}
For mean-centred observations, \textbf{Principal Component Analysis}
chooses $K$ perpendicular unit directions and each observation's
weights to minimise the average squared reconstruction error:
\[
  \min_{C,w_1,\ldots,w_P}
    \frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}\lVert Cw_p-x_p\rVert_2^2,
  \qquad C^\top C=I_K.
\]
The condition $C^\top C=I_K$ requires the chosen columns to be
orthonormal. PCA is \textbf{unsupervised}: it uses the input
observations without needing target labels.
\end{frameddefn}

Read the objective as the procedure we just followed: reconstruct
each point, square its errors on the original coordinates, add those
errors across the dataset, and divide by $P$ to get the average.
The minimisation asks which directions and weights give the smallest
result. A point cloud close to a line or plane may have a positive
minimum; the exact reconstruction in our example is a special case.

\paragraph{For fixed directions, we already know the best weights.}
Once we choose an orthonormal $C$, the previous chapter gives
\[
  w_p=C^\top x_p,\qquad
  \hat{x}_p=Cw_p=CC^\top x_p.
\]
So we do not need to search for the weights independently. For each
candidate $C$, calculate them with dot products, reconstruct the
observations, and measure the average error.

Substituting these best weights into the objective gives a score
that depends only on the chosen directions:
\[
  g(C)=\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}
    \lVert CC^\top x_p-x_p\rVert_2^2,
  \qquad C^\top C=I_K.
\]
Here $g(C)$ is the average squared reconstruction error after using
that choice of directions as well as possible. Change $C$, and both
the best weights and the reconstructions change with it.
PCA seeks the smallest score among all valid choices of $C$.

We now have a way to judge directions by what they lose. Next, we
look at how much the observations spread along a direction, and
connect that spread to the reconstruction error.

\clearpage
\section{Measuring variation along a direction}

We now connect reconstruction error to how much the observations
spread along a chosen direction. First consider keeping just one
direction $c$, so $C=c$ and each encoding is a single number
$w_p=c^\top x_p$. We compare unit vectors: otherwise simply
doubling $c$ would double the coordinates and make their spread look
larger without changing the direction.

\begin{frameddefn}{Variance along a unit direction}\label{def:pca-directional-variance}
Let $c$ have length $1$. Because the observations are centred, their
encoded coordinates $w_p=c^\top x_p$ also have mean $0$.
Their empirical variance, with the $1/P$ convention used here, is
\[
  V(c)=\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}w_p^2
      =\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}\bigl(c^\top x_p\bigr)^2.
\]
Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean. Here that
mean is zero, so each deviation is the encoded coordinate itself.
Larger $V(c)$ means greater spread.
\end{frameddefn}

\begin{framedexample}{Computing the spread in the two directions}\label{ex:pca-variance-choice}
Use the same three observations and compare the unit directions
\[
  c_h=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  c_d=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The diagonal vector $(1,1)^\top$ has length $\sqrt2$. Dividing by
$\sqrt2$ makes its length $1$, so both choices use the same unit of
distance. For $x_3=(2,2)^\top$, its diagonal coordinate is
\[
  w_3=c_d^\top x_3
     =\frac2{\sqrt2}+\frac2{\sqrt2}=2\sqrt2.
\]
Encoding all three observations gives
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Direction} & $w_1$ & $w_2$ & $w_3$ \\
\midrule
Horizontal & $-2$ & $0$ & $2$ \\
Diagonal & $-2\sqrt2$ & $0$ & $2\sqrt2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Both rows have mean zero. Squaring and averaging gives
\[
  V(c_h)=\frac{(-2)^2+0^2+2^2}{3}=\frac83,
\]
\[
  V(c_d)=\frac{(-2\sqrt2)^2+0^2+(2\sqrt2)^2}{3}=\frac{16}{3}.
\]
The diagonal retains more of the original spread. Its negative and
positive coordinates describe opposite sides of the same line.
\end{framedexample}

\clearpage
\section{Why maximum variance gives minimum error}

For a unit direction $c$, write one observation as the sum of its
reconstruction $\hat{x}=cw$, where $w=c^\top x$, and its residual
$r=x-\hat{x}$. The residual is perpendicular to the chosen line.
The original vector, its reconstruction, and its residual therefore
form a right triangle, as in the horizontal projection diagram in
Example~\ref{ex:pca-reconstruction-choice}.

By Pythagoras, the squared lengths satisfy
\[
  \lVert x\rVert_2^2=\lVert\hat{x}\rVert_2^2+\lVert r\rVert_2^2.
\]
Since $c$ has length $1$, the squared length of $\hat{x}=cw$ is
$w^2$. Thus
\[
  \lVert r\rVert_2^2=\lVert x\rVert_2^2-w^2
                   =\lVert x\rVert_2^2-(c^\top x)^2.
\]
For example, projecting $(2,2)^\top$ horizontally gives
$\hat{x}=(2,0)^\top$ and $r=(0,2)^\top$: the squared lengths are
$8=4+4$.

\subsection{From one point to the whole dataset}

Call the average squared reconstruction error $E(c)$. Applying the
same identity to every observation and averaging gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
  E(c)
    &=\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}
        \lVert x_p-cc^\top x_p\rVert_2^2 \\
    &=\underbrace{\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}
        \lVert x_p\rVert_2^2}_{\text{fixed by the data}}
      -\underbrace{\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}
        \bigl(c^\top x_p\bigr)^2}_{V(c):\ \text{variance retained}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first term stays the same when we change direction. Increasing
the second term therefore decreases the reconstruction error by
exactly the same amount.

For our three observations, the fixed term is
\[
  \frac{\lVert(-2,-2)^\top\rVert_2^2+\lVert(0,0)^\top\rVert_2^2
        +\lVert(2,2)^\top\rVert_2^2}{3}
  =\frac{8+0+8}{3}=\frac{16}{3}.
\]
The two choices consequently give
\[
  E(c_h)=\frac{16}{3}-\frac83=\frac83,\qquad
  E(c_d)=\frac{16}{3}-\frac{16}{3}=0.
\]
These are precisely the errors computed from the reconstructed points.

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}
\begin{frameddefn}{First principal direction}\label{def:pca-first-direction}
The first principal direction is a unit vector $c$ that maximises
the variance $V(c)$ of the centred observations along it.
Equivalently, it minimises the average squared error $E(c)$ of
reconstruction using one direction.
\end{frameddefn}

\chapter{Optimization}\label{chap:optimization}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!20,colframe=yellow!60!black,
  boxrule=0.4pt,arc=1mm,left=5pt,right=5pt,top=3pt,bottom=3pt,

\small\textbf{Start of lecture 03 (Spinelli)}

\smallskip
In the chapter on Data Representation, we chose weights to make a
reconstruction error as small as possible. Machine learning uses the
same idea: a loss function scores the error for each choice of a
model's weights, and minimizing it selects the weights with the best
fit according to that score. We now study how to minimize or maximize
a function, beginning with one adjustable number $w$.

\section{What are we trying to optimize?}

Let $g(w)$ assign a numerical score to the choice $w$. We vary $w$
to find the smallest or largest value of $g$, depending on the task.

\begin{frameddefn}{Objective function and optimization}\label{def:optimization-objective}
The \emph{objective function} is the function whose value we want to
minimize or maximize. For an objective $g(w)$, we write
\[
  \min_w g(w)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\max_w g(w).
\]
The subscript $w$ identifies the input we are allowed to change.
When we minimize an error or a cost, the objective is also called a
\emph{loss function} or a \emph{cost function}.
\end{frameddefn}

We use $w_*$ for an input that achieves the desired optimum. Thus
$w_*$ is the chosen input, and $g(w_*)$ is its score. For minimization,
$w_*=\argmin_w g(w)$ means ``choose an input at which $g$ is
smallest''. For maximization, $\argmax$ has the corresponding meaning
with ``largest''. More than one input may achieve the same best score.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\section{Global minima and maxima}

A global optimum must be best among \emph{all} the inputs we are
considering. In the following examples, $w$ can be any real number.

\begin{frameddefn}{Global minimum and global maximum}\label{def:global-optimum}
An input $w_*$ is a \emph{global minimum point} of $g$ if
\[
  g(w_*)\leq g(w)\qquad\text{for every }w.
\]
It is a \emph{global maximum point} if
\[
  g(w_*)\geq g(w)\qquad\text{for every }w.
\]
The values $g(w_*)$ are called the global minimum value and the
global maximum value, respectively.
\end{frameddefn}

\Needspace{22\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{The bottom of a parabola}
Suppose we want to choose $w$ to minimize $g(w)=w^2$. The horizontal
axis shows the input $w$; the vertical axis shows the function value.

For example, $g(-2)=4$, $g(0)=0$, and $g(2)=4$.
Every square is nonnegative, so $g(0)=0\leq w^2$ for every $w$.
Therefore $w_*=0$ is the global minimum point, and the minimum value
is $0$.

If we turn the curve upside down, the function becomes $-g(w)=-w^2$.
Every value is now at most $0$, so the same input $w_*=0$ is its
global maximum point.
\end{framedexample}

This change of sign works for any objective. If one input gives a
larger value of $g$, it gives a smaller value of $-g$. We can therefore
solve a maximization problem by minimizing the negative function:
\[
  \argmax_w g(w)=\argmin_w\bigl(-g(w)\bigr),\qquad
  \max_w g(w)=-\min_w\bigl(-g(w)\bigr).
\]
The first equality identifies the optimizing inputs; the second
relates the optimum values.

\Needspace{35\baselineskip}
\section{Local minima and maxima}

Now imagine a curve with several valleys of different depths.
The bottom of one valley is lower than the points immediately around
it, even when another valley is deeper. This motivates a comparison
with \emph{nearby} inputs.

\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,trim=40pt 48pt 70pt 55pt,clip]{assets/andrew-ng-cost-landscape.pdf}
  \par\smallskip
  {\small\textit{A function with several valleys. Source:
  \href{https://d396qusza40orc.cloudfront.net/ml/docs/slides/Lecture2.pdf}
  {Andrew Ng, Machine Learning, Lecture 2, slide 25}.}}
\end{center}

Here the function has two inputs: the adjustable parameters $\theta_0$
and $\theta_1$ on the horizontal axes. The height $J(\theta_0,\theta_1)$
is the value we want to minimise; $J$ plays the same role as $g$ in
our notation. The black path shows successive downhill steps into
the valley on the right. The valley in front is deeper: being lower
than nearby points does not necessarily mean having the lowest value
overall.

\begin{frameddefn}{Local minimum and local maximum}\label{def:local-optimum}
An input $w_*$ is a \emph{local minimum point} if
\[
  g(w_*)\leq g(w)\qquad\text{for all }w\text{ sufficiently near }w_*.
\]
It is a \emph{local maximum point} if the inequality is reversed.
Here ``sufficiently near'' means that the comparison holds throughout
some small interval around $w_*$.
\end{frameddefn}

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\section{Why these are called zero-order conditions}

The global and local definitions compare values of $g$ directly.
They are called \emph{zero-order conditions} because they use the
function itself. First-order conditions use first derivatives, which
describe the slope of the graph. Second-order conditions use second
derivatives, which describe how that slope changes. The definitions
tell us what it means to be a minimum or maximum; derivatives will
help us find candidate optimum points without comparing every
possible input individually.

\clearpage
\section{Using the derivative to find candidate optima}

We now want to find possible minimum points by solving an equation.
With one adjustable number $w$, we use the derivative $g'(w)$, also
written $\frac{\mathrm{d}g}{\mathrm{d}w}$. It tells us how $g$ changes
when we change $w$ slightly. On the graph, it is the slope of the
\emph{tangent line}: the straight line that follows the direction
of the curve at the point we are examining.

If $g'(w)>0$, moving slightly to the right increases $g$.
If $g'(w)<0$, the same movement decreases $g$.
If $g'(w)=0$, the tangent is horizontal. Let us see what happens
at the bottom of a parabola.

\Needspace{25\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{A horizontal tangent at the minimum}
Suppose we want to minimise $g(w)=w^2+1$. The blue curve shows
the objective, and the green line is its tangent at $w=0$.

\]
This point is a global minimum because $w^2+1\geq1$ for every $w$,

\end{framedexample}

Assume that the derivative exists and that we can move a little
to either side of the input. At a local minimum, neither direction
can lower $g$. A nonzero slope would let us lower it by moving in
one of those directions. The slope must therefore be zero.

\begin{frameddefn}{Stationary point and first-order condition}\label{def:first-order-scalar}
An input $w_*$ is a \emph{stationary point} if
\[
  g'(w_*)=0.
\]
For a differentiable function, every local minimum or local maximum
inside the input domain satisfies this equation. It is called the
\emph{first-order condition}. Here ``inside'' means that small moves
to both sides are allowed.
\end{frameddefn}

\clearpage
\section{Several weights: a system of first-order equations}

We now make the same search with several adjustable weights.
Write them as the vector $w=(w_1,\ldots,w_N)^\top$, where $N$ is
the number of weights. The objective $g(w)$ still returns a single
score, but that score now depends on all $N$ entries.

To check the effect of one weight, vary $w_j$ while keeping the
others fixed. The resulting rate of change is the \emph{partial
derivative} $\frac{\partial g}{\partial w_j}$. The index $j$ identifies
the weight being varied, from $1$ to $N$.

\begin{frameddefn}{Gradient}\label{def:optimization-gradient}
For a differentiable function $g$ of $N$ weights, the \emph{gradient}
collects its $N$ partial derivatives in one column vector:
\[
  \nabla g(w)=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_1}(w)\\[4pt]
    \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_2}(w)\\
    \vdots\\
    \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_N}(w)
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The $j$th entry measures how $g$ changes when we vary $w_j$ while
keeping the other weights fixed.
\end{frameddefn}

At a differentiable local minimum inside the input domain, every
entry must be zero. Otherwise, changing one weight in the appropriate
direction would lower the score.

\begin{frameddefn}{First-order system}\label{def:first-order-system}
A stationary point $w_*$ satisfies the $N$ equations
\[
  \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_1}(w_*)=0,\\[5pt]
    \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_2}(w_*)=0,\\
    \hfill\vdots\\
    \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_N}(w_*)=0.
  \end{cases}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \nabla g(w_*)=\mathbf{0}_{N\times1}.
\]
The right-hand side is a column vector of $N$ zeros. The equations
set every partial derivative to zero, not the value of $g$.
\end{frameddefn}

When we can solve these equations directly, we obtain an
\emph{analytic solution}, also called a \emph{closed-form solution}.
The next example shows the calculation with two weights.

\Needspace{34\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{Two weights, two equations}
Suppose we want to minimise
\[
  g(w_1,w_2)=(w_1-1)^2+(w_2+2)^2+1.
\]
The surface is a bowl. The green plane is its horizontal tangent
at the green point $(w_1,w_2)=(1,-2)$.

With several weights, a zero gradient can also identify a point

\begin{frameddefn}{Saddle point}\label{def:saddle-point}
A \emph{saddle point} of a differentiable function is a stationary

\end{frameddefn}

\clearpage
\section{Approximating a function near one point}

Solving $g'(w)=0$ can become difficult when $g$ is complicated.
Another useful way to study the objective is to replace a small part
of its graph with a simpler shape: its tangent line. This approximation
helps us predict how a small change in a weight will affect the score.

Choose a starting input $w_0$. We know the value $g(w_0)$ and the
slope $g'(w_0)$ there. Moving from $w_0$ to a nearby input $w$ changes
the input by $w-w_0$. Multiplying this change by the slope gives the
predicted change in the function.

\begin{frameddefn}{First-order Taylor approximation}\label{def:first-order-taylor}
The \emph{first-order Taylor approximation} of $g$ at $w_0$ is
\[
  h(w)=g(w_0)+g'(w_0)(w-w_0).
\]
The function $h$ is the tangent line. It has the same value and the
same slope as $g$ at $w_0$. For inputs close to $w_0$, we use
$g(w)\approx h(w)$.
\end{frameddefn}

\clearpage
\subsection{From a tangent line to a tangent plane}

With $N$ weights, the starting input $w_0$ is a vector.
The displacement $w-w_0$ contains the change in each weight.
Each partial derivative predicts the effect of its corresponding
change, and we add these contributions:
\[
  \nabla g(w_0)^\top(w-w_0)
  =\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial g}{\partial w_j}(w_0)
    (w_j-w_{0,j}).
\]
Here $w_{0,j}$ is the $j$th entry of the starting vector $w_0$.
The product on the left is a dot product: it multiplies matching
entries of the two vectors and sums the results.

\begin{frameddefn}{Tangent hyperplane}\label{def:tangent-hyperplane}
For a differentiable function of several weights, the first-order
Taylor approximation at $w_0$ is
\[
  h(w)=g(w_0)+\nabla g(w_0)^\top(w-w_0).
\]
Its graph is the \emph{tangent hyperplane}. It passes through the
point $(w_0,g(w_0))$ and has the same partial derivatives as $g$
there. For nearby inputs, $g(w)\approx h(w)$.
\end{frameddefn}

For one input, the graph is a line with two coordinates: the weight
and the function value. For two inputs, it is a plane with three
coordinates: two weights and the function value. In general, this
flat graph has dimension $N$ in a space with $N+1$ coordinates.

We can collect the constants and write the same model as
\[
  h(w)=a+b^\top w,\qquad
  a=g(w_0)-\nabla g(w_0)^\top w_0,\quad b=\nabla g(w_0).
\]
The vector $b$ contains the slopes, while the scalar $a$ sets the
height of the graph when $w=0$. This is an \emph{affine hyperplane}:
the constant term allows it to be shifted away from the origin.

  h(w_1,w_2)=3+2(w_1-2)+2(w_2+1)=1+2w_1+2w_2.
\]
In the form $h(w)=a+b^\top w$, we have $a=1$ and $b=(2,2)^\top$.
Checking the starting point gives $h(2,-1)=3$, exactly as required.

Changing both weights by $0.1$ gives $(2.1,-0.9)^\top$.
The plane predicts $3.4$, while the actual function value is
$1.1^2+1.1^2+1=3.42$.
\end{framedexample}

\section{Choosing the direction of fastest change}

The tangent model helps us choose how to change several weights
together. With one weight, we can move left or right; the sign of
the slope determines which way increases the function. With two
or more weights, there are infinitely many directions to consider.

Let $d=(d_1,\ldots,d_N)^\top$ describe a direction. To compare
directions fairly, give them the same Euclidean length:
\[
  \|d\|=\sqrt{d_1^2+\cdots+d_N^2}=1.
\]
Such a vector is called a \emph{unit direction}. If $\alpha>0$ is
the distance we choose to move, the new weights are $w_0+\alpha d$.
All the directions being compared then produce steps of length
$\alpha$.

For the affine model $h(w)=a+b^\top w$, this move gives
\[
  h(w_0+\alpha d)=a+b^\top w_0+\alpha b^\top d.
\]
The first two terms are fixed. The predicted change is
$\alpha b^\top d$, so the direction with the largest dot product
$b^\top d$ gives the largest increase; the smallest gives the largest
decrease.

Assume $b\ne0$ and let $\theta$ be the angle between $b$ and $d$.
The dot product satisfies
\[
  b^\top d=\|b\|\,\|d\|\cos\theta=\|b\|\cos\theta.
\]
The length $\|b\|$ is fixed. The largest value occurs when
$\cos\theta=1$, so $d$ points along $b$. The smallest occurs when
$\cos\theta=-1$, so $d$ points opposite to $b$.

\begin{frameddefn}{Steepest ascent and steepest descent}\label{def:steepest-directions}
At an input $w_0$ where $\nabla g(w_0)\ne0$, the Euclidean unit
directions of fastest local increase and decrease are
\[
  d_{\mathrm{ascent}}=
  \frac{\nabla g(w_0)}{\|\nabla g(w_0)\|},\qquad
  d_{\mathrm{descent}}=
  -\frac{\nabla g(w_0)}{\|\nabla g(w_0)\|}.
\]
The largest rate of increase per unit distance is $\|\nabla g(w_0)\|$.
The gradient itself points in the ascent direction, and its negative
points in the descent direction. Dividing by the length makes these
vectors unit directions.
\end{frameddefn}

The tangent model has the same slopes as the original function at
$w_0$, so these are also the fastest \emph{local} directions for $g$.
A sufficiently small step opposite to a nonzero gradient lowers $g$;
a large step can increase it. Choosing a direction and choosing how
far to move are therefore separate parts of the search.

At a stationary point, the gradient is zero and the tangent model
is horizontal. It gives no preferred direction: we must examine more
than this first-order approximation to understand the function there.

\Needspace{32\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{Equal step lengths, different changes}
For $g(w_1,w_2)=(w_1-1)^2+(w_2+2)^2+1$ at $w_0=(2,-1)^\top$,
the tangent model is $h(w)=1+2w_1+2w_2$ and $b=(2,2)^\top$.
Compare the following unit directions. The dot product gives the
change in $h$ per unit distance.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
Unit direction $d$ & $b^\top d$ & Effect on $h$\\
\midrule
$(1,0)^\top$ & $2$ & increase\\[3pt]
$(0,1)^\top$ & $2$ & increase\\[3pt]
$(1,-1)^\top/\sqrt{2}$ & $0$ & no change\\[3pt]
$(1,1)^\top/\sqrt{2}$ & $2\sqrt{2}$ & fastest increase\\[3pt]
$(-1,-1)^\top/\sqrt{2}$ & $-2\sqrt{2}$ & fastest decrease\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The gradient length is $\sqrt{2^2+2^2}=2\sqrt{2}$. Dividing the
negative gradient by this length gives
\[
  d_{\mathrm{descent}}=
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
A step of length $\alpha$ in this direction gives the new weights
\[
  w_0+\alpha d_{\mathrm{descent}}
  =\begin{pmatrix}2-\alpha/\sqrt{2}\\-1-\alpha/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The tangent model predicts the value $3-2\sqrt{2}\alpha$.
Substituting the new weights into the actual objective gives
\[
  g(w_0+\alpha d_{\mathrm{descent}})
  =2\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2+1
  =3-2\sqrt{2}\alpha+\alpha^2.
\]
For $\alpha=0.1$, the predicted value is about $2.717$, and the
actual value is about $2.727$. Both are below the starting value $3$.
The extra $\alpha^2$ term shows the part that the tangent model
does not capture.
\end{framedexample}

\section{Level curves and the gradient}

We can also view a two-weight objective from above. Instead of
drawing its height, we connect inputs with the same function value,
as a map connects places at the same altitude.

\begin{frameddefn}{Level set and level curve}\label{def:level-set}
For a fixed score $c$, the \emph{level set} of $g$ is
\[
  \{w:g(w)=c\}.
\]
With two inputs, these sets are usually drawn as \emph{level curves}.
Every point on one curve has the same score $c$. Different curves
correspond to different scores.
\end{frameddefn}

\Needspace{32\baselineskip}
\begin{framedexample}{Reading the gradient on a contour map}
For $g(w_1,w_2)=(w_1-1)^2+(w_2+2)^2+1$, the level curves are
circles centred at the minimum $(1,-2)^\top$, where $g=1$.
The black point $w_0=(2,-1)^\top$ has score $3$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small,x=1cm,y=1cm]
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!35] (-1.4,0)--(3.8,0) node[right] {$w_1$};
  \draw[-{Stealth},black!35] (0,-4.4)--(0,0.7) node[above] {$w_2$};
  \foreach \rr in {1,1.414214,2}
    {\draw[NotesBlue!70,thick] (1,-2) circle (\rr);}
  \node[fill=NotesPaper,inner sep=1pt,right] at (1.6,-2.8) {$g=2$};
  \node[fill=NotesPaper,inner sep=1pt,right] at (1.2,-3.4) {$g=3$};
  \node[fill=NotesPaper,inner sep=1pt,right] at (2.6,-3.2) {$g=5$};
  \fill[green!60!black] (1,-2) circle (2.5pt);
  \node[below left] at (1,-2) {$g=1$};
  \draw[black!55,dashed] (1.4,-0.4)--(2.6,-1.6);
  \draw[black!55,thin] (1.2,0.05)--(1.4,-0.4);
  \node[above left] at (1.4,0.05) {tangent};
  \draw[-{Stealth},orange!90!black,thick] (2,-1)--(2.8,-0.2);
  \node[orange!90!black,right] at (2.8,-0.2) {ascent};
  \draw[-{Stealth},green!60!black,thick] (2,-1)--(1.45,-1.55);
  \node[green!50!black,below right] at (1.45,-1.55) {descent};
  \draw[black!70] (2.12,-0.88)--(2.24,-1)--(2.12,-1.12);
  \fill[black] (2,-1) circle (2.5pt);
  \node[above left] at (2,-1) {$w_0$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

The gradient $(2,2)^\top$ points outward, towards larger scores;
its negative points inward, towards smaller scores.
The dashed tangent direction $t=(1,-1)^\top$ is perpendicular to it:
\[
  \nabla g(w_0)^\top t=2\cdot1+2\cdot(-1)=0.
\]
Moving along the level curve keeps the score constant, so the
first-order change in its tangent direction is zero.
\end{framedexample}

Where the gradient is nonzero, it is perpendicular to the level
curve. In higher dimensions, it is perpendicular to the surface
of constant function value.
